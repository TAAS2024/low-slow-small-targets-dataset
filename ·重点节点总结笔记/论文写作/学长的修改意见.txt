Review Report
1. Overall Recommendation

Reject

The manuscript addresses an important and timely problem—synthetic data generation for low-slow-small (LSS) target detection—and presents an end-to-end framework combining LLM-based semantic parsing, LoRA/Stable Diffusion generation, ControlNet-based spatial conditioning, multi-stage validation, and feedback-driven refinement. The topic is broadly relevant to Neurocomputing, particularly in the areas of deep generative models, computer vision, and intelligent data generation.

However, in its current form, I do not consider the manuscript to provide sufficient algorithmic novelty, theoretical substantiation, or experimental evidence for publication in Neurocomputing. The central contribution is largely an integration of existing components rather than a demonstrably new neural-network architecture or learning algorithm. More importantly, the principal claims concerning the closed-loop data flywheel, multi-stage validation, scalability, and downstream detection utility are not adequately validated. The experimental study is based on only 20 generated RGB-IR pairs, while several key ablations are explicitly deferred to future work.

There are also serious internal inconsistencies in the reported experimental results. For example, Section 4.3 reports a mean position error of 0.40 pixels, whereas Table 2 reports 1.130 ± 0.325 pixels; similarly, the text reports a scale entropy of 2.29 bits, while Table 2 gives 2.88 bits. These inconsistencies substantially undermine confidence in the quantitative evaluation.

In my view, the manuscript would require a fundamental reworking of its contribution claims and a substantially expanded experimental validation rather than a conventional revision.

2. Confidence Level

5/5

The manuscript provides sufficient methodological and experimental detail to assess its principal claims. The major concerns arise directly from the reported methodology, quantitative results, experimental scale, ablation design, and internal inconsistencies, rather than from missing contextual information.

3. Summary of the Paper

This paper proposes a Closed-loop Data Flywheel Framework (CDFF) for automated generation of spatially controlled, pixel-aligned RGB–infrared datasets for low-slow-small (LSS) target detection. The framework consists of a Generator Agent and a Validator Agent. The Generator Agent contains modules for LLM-based semantic parsing, real-world background retrieval, monocular depth and segmentation extraction, LoRA-based drone synthesis using Stable Diffusion 1.5, ControlNet-guided spatial repainting, and RGB-to-IR conversion. An automated marker injection mechanism derives spatial conditioning maps from background depth, segmentation, and generated drone silhouettes, thereby avoiding manually prepared spatial conditions.

The Validator Agent performs nine stages of rule-based and model-based quality checks covering semantic consistency, background relevance, geometric consistency, object integrity, repositioning precision, image quality, scene consistency, trajectory continuity, and detection validity. Validation failures are routed to corresponding buffers that can trigger LoRA adaptation, ControlNet parameter adjustment, or prompt refinement. A curriculum hardening mechanism and several physical constraints are introduced to regulate the feedback loop.

The experimental evaluation uses a 28,000-image background pool but only 20 generated RGB-IR pairs for end-to-end validation. The reported evaluation focuses on spatial alignment, BRISQUE-based image quality, diversity, and validator correctness. The manuscript reports sub-pixel positioning, approximately 2% scale error, 90% BRISQUE-based acceptance, and exact RGB–IR annotation alignment. Downstream object-detection performance is not experimentally evaluated, and several proposed ablations are left for future work.

4. Strengths
4.1. The problem is relevant and practically meaningful

The manuscript identifies a genuine bottleneck in LSS detection: collecting sufficiently diverse, accurately annotated, multimodal training data is difficult. The focus on small targets, diverse environmental conditions, and RGB–IR alignment is relevant to computer vision and intelligent sensing applications within the scope of Neurocomputing.

4.2. The overall pipeline is logically organized

The separation into generation, validation, and feedback stages is conceptually clear. The manuscript provides a relatively detailed description of the processing chain, including the semantic specification, background retrieval, depth/segmentation processing, marker generation, diffusion-based repainting, modality conversion, and validation.

The modular architecture could potentially facilitate reproducibility and component-level evaluation if the implementation and complete parameter specifications were made available.

4.3. Automated spatial conditioning is a reasonable engineering contribution

The marker injection strategy is one of the more concrete contributions. Instead of requiring manually constructed ControlNet conditions for every sample, the method derives the depth and segmentation conditions algorithmically from the background and the generated object silhouette. The spatial sampling constraints are also explicitly specified.

This addresses an actual scalability problem in manually controlled synthetic-data generation.

4.4. The manuscript recognizes important limitations

The authors appropriately acknowledge that the evaluation is only proof-of-concept scale, that the IR representation is not physically accurate, that the background pool is static, and that only drone targets are currently generated. This is preferable to ignoring obvious limitations.

4.5. The paper has a potentially interesting quality-control perspective

The idea of assigning different failure types to different validation stages could be useful in a production-oriented synthetic-data pipeline. The engineered tests for V1–V9 at least demonstrate that the implemented validators can respond to deliberately constructed failure cases.

4.6. Broad alignment with Neurocomputing

The manuscript uses diffusion models, LoRA, ControlNet, CLIP, LLMs, and computer-vision models, and applies them to a visual perception problem. Thus, the topic is within the broad application scope of neural computation and intelligent perception.

However, scope relevance should not be confused with sufficient scientific novelty, which remains the main issue.

5. Major Concerns / Weaknesses
Major Concern 1 — The claimed methodological novelty is insufficiently separated from existing components

Severity: Critical

Location

Sections 1.4 and 3.1–3.7.

Problem

The manuscript presents the CDFF as a "dual-agent adversarial architecture" and describes the marker injection, nine-stage validation, curriculum hardening, and feedback mechanism as major methodological contributions. However, most underlying components are established techniques:

LLM semantic parsing;
CLIP-based retrieval;
MiDaS depth estimation;
heuristic segmentation;
LoRA;
Stable Diffusion;
ControlNet;
DDIM;
pseudo-color transformation;
BRISQUE;
rule-based validation;
hard-negative fine-tuning.

The principal novelty appears to lie in system-level orchestration and engineering integration, rather than in a new neural architecture, learning objective, optimization algorithm, or theoretically grounded learning mechanism.

For a journal such as Neurocomputing, this distinction is important. A framework that combines established models can certainly be publishable, but it needs either:

a clearly novel learning formulation;
a new neural architecture;
a theoretically motivated training/optimization mechanism; or
compelling empirical evidence demonstrating that the proposed integration produces a substantial capability not achievable through existing approaches.

At present, the manuscript does not establish this sufficiently.

Recommendation

The authors should explicitly distinguish:

new algorithms proposed by the authors;
existing pretrained models used as components;
engineering orchestration;
empirically demonstrated advantages.

The contribution statement should be substantially narrowed if no new learning algorithm is introduced. In particular, the terms "adversarial architecture," "flywheel," "co-evolve," and "self-improving" should not be used as substitutes for demonstrated learning novelty.

Major Concern 2 — The central closed-loop claim is not experimentally demonstrated

Severity: Critical

Location

Sections 1.3, 1.4, 3.7, 4.5, and Conclusion.

Problem

The manuscript claims that CDFF provides sustained quality improvement through closed-loop adversarial feedback and curriculum hardening. The abstract states that the generator and validator "co-evolve" and that the framework enables "iterative quality bootstrapping."

However, the paper does not provide convincing quantitative evidence for this claim.

The manuscript itself states that two critical ablations—disabling closed-loop feedback and fixing validator thresholds—are planned but not experimentally evaluated.

This is not a minor omission. These experiments directly test the central claimed mechanism.

Recommendation

At minimum, the authors should compare:

Open-loop generation;
Closed-loop feedback without curriculum hardening;
Closed-loop + curriculum hardening;
Full CDFF.

Across multiple generation cycles, the authors should report:

acceptance rate versus generation cycle;
BRISQUE;
position error;
scale error;
IoU;
failure-category distribution;
diversity;
number of feedback updates;
LoRA/ControlNet update frequency.

A learning curve showing improvement over cycles is particularly important.

Without such evidence, the "flywheel" mechanism remains primarily a design hypothesis rather than a validated scientific contribution.

Major Concern 3 — The downstream detection utility, which is arguably the most important application claim, is not evaluated

Severity: Critical

Location

Sections 1.3, 4.2, 4.3, 4.5, and Conclusion.

Problem

The paper is framed as a framework for LSS target detection dataset generation, but it does not actually demonstrate whether the generated data improves LSS detection.

The manuscript explicitly defines downstream detection utility as a research question but then treats it only as a future consideration.

This creates a serious mismatch between the stated objective and the experimental evidence.

High BRISQUE quality, low geometric error, or perfect RGB–IR annotation alignment does not establish that generated samples are useful for training detectors.

Synthetic images can have excellent geometric fidelity while exhibiting severe synthetic-to-real domain gaps. Conversely, perceptually imperfect samples can sometimes provide useful augmentation.

Recommendation

The revised manuscript should include a downstream detection experiment, for example:

real-data-only training;
real + Copy-Paste;
real + diffusion-generated data;
real + CDFF-generated data;
optionally, competing synthetic-data methods.

Evaluation should include at least:

mAP@0.5;
mAP@0.5:0.95;
precision;
recall;
small-object AP/AR;
RGB-only and RGB–IR settings where applicable.

More importantly, the test set must contain real-world images that are excluded from all generation and tuning procedures.

Without downstream detection evaluation, the manuscript demonstrates generation quality but does not establish the claimed contribution to LSS detection.

Major Concern 4 — The sample size of 20 is far too small for the strength of the claims

Severity: Critical

Location

Section 4.1 and Table 1.

Problem

The core end-to-end experiment consists of only 20 RGB–IR pairs, despite the manuscript emphasizing scalable and potentially large-volume dataset generation.

Twenty samples cannot provide convincing evidence for:

robustness;
diversity;
generalization;
scalability;
stability across scenes;
low-light robustness;
failure rates;
closed-loop improvement;
distributional fidelity.

For example, the reported 90% acceptance rate corresponds to 18/20 samples. Statistically, this is a very weak basis for claiming robust quality.

The diversity analysis is similarly limited because 20 distinct condition tuples are almost guaranteed to produce a high uniqueness count if the sampling procedure intentionally enforces distinct conditions.

Recommendation

The evaluation should be substantially expanded. Even if full-scale generation is computationally expensive, the authors should provide a statistically meaningful evaluation across:

multiple environmental categories;
multiple target scales;
multiple target positions;
multiple viewpoints;
illumination conditions;
weather conditions;
different backgrounds;
multiple random seeds.

Confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals should be reported where appropriate.

Major Concern 5 — There are serious internal inconsistencies in the quantitative results

Severity: Critical

Location

Sections 4.3 and Table 2.

Problem

This is one of the most serious issues in the manuscript.

Section 4.3 reports:

mean bounding-box position error = 0.40 pixels ± 0.28

and claims every sample achieves sub-pixel accuracy.

However, Table 2 reports:

Position error = 1.130 ± 0.325 pixels, minimum 0.510, maximum 1.666.

These values are incompatible. If the maximum error is 1.666 pixels, the statement that every sample achieves sub-pixel accuracy is false.

There is another inconsistency:

Section 4.3: scale entropy = 2.29 bits;
Table 2: scale entropy = 2.88 bits.

The abstract and conclusion also repeat the 0.40-pixel result.

Recommendation

The authors must:

identify the correct dataset/result;
regenerate all statistics directly from the raw experimental outputs;
ensure every table, figure, abstract, conclusion, and discussion uses the same values;
provide the raw per-sample results in supplementary material.

This issue must be resolved before the scientific conclusions can be trusted.

Major Concern 6 — The "IR dataset" is not actually a physically meaningful infrared dataset

Severity: Major

Location

Section 3.5.

Problem

The RGB-to-IR transformation is simply a deterministic pseudo-colour transformation of RGB luminance:

$$ L=1-(0.299R+0.587G+0.114B)/255 $$

followed by channel reconstruction.

The authors correctly acknowledge that this does not model:

temperature;
emissivity;
atmospheric attenuation;
sensor noise;
thermal blooming.

Nevertheless, the manuscript repeatedly frames the output as an RGB-IR dataset and suggests applicability to multimodal detection.

This is potentially misleading. Pixel alignment is guaranteed, but cross-modal physical validity is not.

Moreover, the assertion that a "warm drone against a cooler sky background" is represented is not justified merely from RGB luminance inversion.

Recommendation

The terminology should be changed to something such as:

synthetic pseudo-thermal representation

unless the authors can establish physical or empirical correspondence to real infrared imagery.

More importantly, the authors should evaluate whether detectors trained on these pseudo-IR images transfer to real IR test data. Otherwise, the multimodal detection claim remains speculative.

Major Concern 7 — The ablation study is inadequate and statistically weak

Severity: Major

Location

Section 4.5 and Table 3.

Problem

Only one actual ablation is quantitatively reported, using merely three matched pairs.

The other key ablations are not performed.

Three samples are insufficient to support conclusions such as:

"confirms that the depth-guided repainting step ... is responsible for the geometric fidelity"

This causal interpretation is too strong.

Furthermore, the comparison against hard paste is not enough to establish superiority over contemporary spatial-control approaches such as:

ControlNet without the proposed marker construction;
GLIGEN;
InstanceDiffusion;
LayoutDiffusion;
box-conditioned diffusion generation;
Copy-Paste;
potentially newer detection-oriented diffusion generation methods.
Recommendation

A proper ablation matrix should independently evaluate:

no marker injection;
hard paste;
marker injection;
marker injection + depth;
marker injection + segmentation;
marker injection + ControlNet;
multi-stage validation;
closed-loop feedback;
curriculum hardening;
FRRM.

Each experiment should use the same background/target conditions and preferably multiple random seeds.

Major Concern 8 — The validation framework is primarily a collection of manually designed heuristics rather than a demonstrated intelligent validation model

Severity: Major

Location

Sections 3.6 and 4.4.

Problem

The paper describes the Validator Agent as a sophisticated nine-stage validation agent. However, many stages are deterministic rule-based checks:

schema rules;
depth thresholds;
segmentation rules;
geometric thresholds;
histogram KL divergence;
trajectory constraints.

The paper itself describes the validator as consisting of deterministic rule-based checks.

Therefore, the term "Agent" may overstate the neural/intelligent nature of this component.

More importantly, the experiments merely show that deliberately injected defects trigger the corresponding validators. This demonstrates implementation correctness, not validation effectiveness.

Recommendation

The authors should evaluate the validator as a detection/classification system:

true-positive rejection rate;
false-positive rejection rate;
false-negative acceptance rate;
precision;
recall;
F1;
ROC/PR curves where applicable.

A human-labelled benchmark containing both acceptable and defective generated samples would provide substantially stronger evidence.

Major Concern 9 — The causal interpretation of the marker-injection improvement is not justified
Location

Section 4.5.

The manuscript concludes that the improvement is due specifically to depth-guided repainting rather than placement.

However, the experiment compares:

marker injection + ControlNet;
hard paste.

This changes multiple factors simultaneously.

The improvement could arise from:

diffusion-based blending;
ControlNet conditioning;
depth conditioning;
segmentation conditioning;
boundary smoothing;
different compositing procedures.
Recommendation

Use a factorial or controlled ablation:

hard paste;
hard paste + Gaussian boundary;
ControlNet without depth;
ControlNet + depth;
ControlNet + segmentation;
ControlNet + depth + segmentation.

Only then can the contribution of each component be isolated.

Major Concern 10 — Generalization claims are unsupported
Location

Abstract, Sections 3.4 and Conclusion.

The manuscript states that the framework generalizes to UAVs, kites, balloons, and airships.

However, the experiments only evaluate a single drone category.

The statement that the same pipeline "generalise[s] without modification" to other LSS categories is therefore an architectural hypothesis, not an experimentally demonstrated result.

Recommendation

Either:

experimentally evaluate at least two or three additional LSS categories; or
explicitly describe this as potential extensibility rather than demonstrated generalization.
Major Concern 11 — The LLM component lacks an empirical evaluation
Location

Section 3.2.

The semantic parser is described as an LLM-driven component with schema validation and fallback behavior.

However, there is no quantitative evaluation of:

parsing accuracy;
field extraction accuracy;
trajectory extraction accuracy;
invalid-output rate;
ambiguity handling;
robustness to paraphrased descriptions.
Recommendation

Create a benchmark of manually annotated scene descriptions and report field-level accuracy and complete-JSON validity. Also compare the LLM parser with a deterministic template-based parser to establish whether the LLM actually provides measurable value.

Major Concern 12 — Reproducibility remains inadequate
Location

Sections 3.3–3.7 and 4.1.

Although some implementation details are provided, many critical parameters are unspecified or incompletely specified:

exact Stable Diffusion checkpoint;
LoRA rank;
LoRA learning rate;
number of training epochs/steps;
training-set size;
optimizer parameters;
ControlNet checkpoint;
ControlNet conditioning weights;
diffusion sampling steps;
classifier-free guidance scale;
exact BRISQUE threshold;
all validator thresholds;
background retrieval weights;
value of \(N\);
exact acceptance criteria;
hardware configuration;
runtime breakdown.

The paper claims reproducibility while simultaneously stating that large-scale generation is still ongoing.

Recommendation

Provide a complete configuration table and, preferably, release:

source code;
configuration files;
LoRA weights;
generated evaluation samples;
validation annotations;
random seeds;
evaluation scripts.
6. Minor Issues
Terminology is occasionally overstated. Terms such as "adversarial," "co-evolution," "self-improving," and "flywheel" imply a stronger learning mechanism than is experimentally demonstrated.
Equation formatting requires correction. For example, Eq. (6) contains malformed/duplicated symbols in the extracted manuscript. The mathematical notation should be checked against the original source.
Eq. (13) is incorrectly formatted. The union/intersection expression is not clearly rendered in the manuscript.
Variable definitions are incomplete. Symbols such as \(D\), \(S\), \(M\), \(\hat d\), \(c_{fg}\), and \(s_{ref}\) should be formally defined before use.
The notation \(H\times N\) appears to use \(N\) where image width would normally be \(W\). This should be checked.
Table 2 and Section 4.3 must be reconciled, as discussed above.
The manuscript claims 20 unique condition tuples as evidence of diversity, but the generation procedure itself intentionally uses distinct generation conditions. This is therefore a weak diversity measure.
BRISQUE alone is insufficient for evaluating diffusion-generated images, particularly because BRISQUE measures generic perceptual quality rather than task-specific usefulness.
The manuscript says that FID will be reported in future work. If FID is important enough to motivate distributional-quality claims, it should either be evaluated now or the relevant claims should be softened.
The background pool is obtained through web scraping. The manuscript should provide a clearer discussion of copyright/licensing provenance, even though it states that the LoRA data were permissively licensed.
The reference list contains numbering/formatting irregularities, including inconsistent spacing and apparent reference-number formatting problems.
Several references appear to have bibliographic details that should be carefully checked against the original publications.
The statement that ControlNet methods "require manually prepared spatial conditions" is too broad. The novelty claim should distinguish the underlying conditioning method from the proposed automatic condition-generation procedure.
The statement that current datasets "cannot be systematically extended" is too categorical and should be supported more carefully.
The paper should distinguish dataset-generation scalability from computational scalability. A pipeline requiring approximately 90–150 seconds per sample is not necessarily scalable merely because it is automated.
7. Detailed Comments
7.1 Introduction
Strength

The Introduction identifies three concrete challenges:

spatial-control scalability;
quality-assessment limitations;
open-loop generation.

This gives the manuscript a reasonably coherent motivation.

Required improvement

The research-gap claims are currently stronger than the literature evidence supports. Statements such as "has yet to be explored" or "none provides" require systematic literature coverage.

More importantly, the Introduction should answer a fundamental question:

What is the new neural-computational principle introduced by CDFF beyond assembling existing generative and perception models?

At present, the answer is unclear.

The contributions should therefore be rewritten around demonstrated innovations, rather than architectural aspirations.

7.2 Related Work

The Related Work section covers synthetic data generation, spatial control, LSS detection, and quality assessment. This organization is appropriate.

However, the comparison remains largely narrative.

A substantially more useful approach would be a table comparing the proposed method with representative methods such as:

Method	Spatial control	Automatic conditioning	Detection-oriented	RGB–IR	Quality validation	Feedback	Downstream detection
Copy-Paste	Limited	Partial	Yes	No	Limited	No	Yes
ControlNet	Yes	No	Not specific	No	No	No	Indirect
GLIGEN	Yes	Partial	General	No	No	No	Indirect
InstanceDiffusion	Yes	Partial	Yes	No	No	No	Yes
ODGEN	Yes	Partial	Yes	No	Limited	No	Yes
CDFF	Claimed	Yes	Claimed	Claimed	Yes	Claimed	Not evaluated

Such a table would make the novelty boundary much clearer.

7.3 Proposed Method
7.3.1 LLM semantic parsing

The module is technically reasonable, but its necessity is not demonstrated.

A fixed JSON schema with a template-based parser might be sufficient for the current controlled vocabulary. Therefore, the manuscript needs an experiment showing the advantage of LLM parsing.

7.3.2 Background matching

The use of CLIP plus metadata is reasonable. However, the weighted similarity function is not mathematically specified sufficiently.

The manuscript should provide:

exact scoring equation;
weights;
normalization procedure;
retrieval top-\(k\);
tie-breaking;
ablation of CLIP versus metadata.
7.3.3 Depth and segmentation

The reliance on MiDaS and a heuristic segmenter is a potential weakness.

The manuscript itself states that both are provisional solutions.

This is especially problematic because depth and segmentation are fundamental inputs to the claimed spatial-control contribution.

A comparison with at least one stronger depth/segmentation model would help determine whether the proposed pipeline is robust to upstream perception errors.

7.4 Marker Injection

This is arguably the most promising part of the manuscript.

However, the paper currently demonstrates only that the algorithm can generate conditioning maps. It does not sufficiently establish that the proposed maps are superior to existing spatial-conditioning mechanisms.

The authors should therefore evaluate marker injection against:

manually specified ControlNet masks;
automatic bounding-box conditions;
GLIGEN;
InstanceDiffusion;
hard paste.

The evaluation should focus on:

position error;
scale error;
IoU;
boundary quality;
background preservation.
7.5 ControlNet Repainting

The description is detailed but heavily implementation-oriented.

The authors should clarify exactly which part constitutes the new method. If the main novelty is the construction of \(D'\) and \(S'\), then the ControlNet itself should be explicitly treated as a pretrained component rather than as part of the claimed novel architecture.

7.6 RGB-to-IR Conversion

This is currently the weakest methodological component from a scientific perspective.

The proposed transformation guarantees pixel alignment, but this is a trivial consequence of applying a deterministic pixel-wise transformation. Therefore:

Pixel alignment should not be presented as strong evidence of multimodal realism.

The key question is whether the pseudo-IR representation preserves the discriminative characteristics of real infrared imagery.

This requires evaluation against real thermal images.

7.7 Nine-Stage Validation

The concept of distributed validation is interesting, but the paper needs to distinguish between:

validator correctness, and
validator effectiveness.

The engineered tests in Section 4.4 demonstrate that the rules behave according to their implementation. They do not demonstrate that these rules correctly distinguish real high-quality from real low-quality generated samples.

A human-annotated validation set would be much more convincing.

7.8 Closed-Loop Feedback

This is the central weakness of the current manuscript.

The paper proposes a sophisticated feedback system involving three buffers, update thresholds, asymmetric update frequencies, curriculum levels, and rollback.

Yet the actual experimental section does not demonstrate the claimed dynamics.

A central figure should show:

generation cycle → failure accumulation → parameter update → quality improvement → acceptance-rate change

over perhaps 100–500 cycles.

Without this, the proposed "data flywheel" is essentially an architectural specification.

7.9 Experiments

The experimental section should be substantially redesigned.

At minimum, I would expect four evaluation blocks:

A. Generation quality
BRISQUE;
LPIPS;
FID/KID where meaningful;
human evaluation;
geometric fidelity.
B. Spatial controllability
position error;
scale error;
IoU;
boundary error;
background preservation.
C. Closed-loop learning
open-loop versus closed-loop;
curriculum versus no curriculum;
quality versus iteration;
diversity versus iteration.
D. Downstream detection

This is essential:

real-only;
synthetic-only;
real + synthetic;
competing synthetic-data methods;
CDFF.

This would directly establish whether the generated dataset has practical value.

7.10 Statistical Analysis

The current manuscript mostly reports means and standard deviations without explaining repeated trials or random seeds.

For generative models, stochasticity is fundamental.

The authors should report:

number of independent runs;
random seeds;
confidence intervals;
mean ± standard deviation over runs;
statistical significance where appropriate.

Three matched pairs are particularly insufficient for the ablation conclusions.

7.11 Conclusion

The Conclusion currently states several findings as established conclusions even though some are actually hypotheses or future objectives.

For example, the statement that the framework establishes a "reproducible and scalable paradigm" is too strong given the 20-sample evaluation and the absence of large-scale throughput/acceptance statistics.

The conclusion should distinguish clearly between:

experimentally demonstrated findings;
architectural capabilities;
hypotheses;
future work.
8. Additional Comments to the Editor

Recommendation: Reject

The manuscript is within the broad topical scope of Neurocomputing, but I do not believe it currently meets the journal's expected standard of methodological novelty and experimental validation.

The main issue is not that the proposed pipeline is technically uninteresting. The integration of LLM-based specification, diffusion generation, automated spatial conditioning, multi-stage validation, and feedback could potentially form a useful research direction. However, the manuscript currently presents a system architecture and proof-of-concept implementation rather than a sufficiently validated neural-computing contribution.

The most problematic aspects are:

Novelty ambiguity: the framework primarily integrates existing models and heuristics.
No convincing evidence for the claimed closed-loop learning mechanism.
No downstream LSS detection experiment, despite detection-oriented positioning.
Only 20 generated samples are used for the principal evaluation.
Critical ablations are explicitly deferred to future work.
The synthetic IR modality is only pseudo-thermal, with no evidence of transfer to real IR data.
Internal numerical contradictions occur in the main experimental results.
Several conclusions substantially exceed what the experiments demonstrate.

I would therefore not recommend a conventional major revision. A publishable version would require a substantial reconceptualization of the scientific contribution and a new experimental campaign, rather than merely additional explanation or polishing.

If the authors were to substantially revise the work, the most important path would be to reposition the paper around one clearly novel mechanism—most plausibly the automatic spatial-conditioning and closed-loop quality-control mechanism—and then provide rigorous component-wise ablation, multi-cycle closed-loop experiments, statistically meaningful generation evaluation, and, critically, downstream detection performance on an independent real-world LSS benchmark.
